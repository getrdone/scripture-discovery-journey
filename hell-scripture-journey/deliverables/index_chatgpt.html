A Scripture-to-Scripture Journey

What does the Bible really say about hell?

Not whether judgment is real. Not whether fire is serious. The question is narrower: Does the Bible teach that the lost remain consciously alive forever in torment—or that sin finally ends in death and destruction?

This study will not skip the hard texts.Revelation 14:11. Revelation 20:10. Eternal punishment. Unquenchable fire. The undying worm. The rich man and Lazarus. Each belongs in the evidence path.

Start with the simplest contrast

Life or death?

Before reading symbols, begin with the Bible's ordinary words for the two outcomes.

Romans 6:23John 3:161 John 5:11–12

Romans places death opposite God's gift of eternal life. John places perishing opposite everlasting life. That creates the first question the rest of the study must answer: if every person is naturally immortal, why is eternal life repeatedly presented as a gift?

First observation: the Bible's baseline contrast is not eternal happiness versus eternal life in pain. It is life versus death.
Human nature

What happens when a person dies?

Genesis 2:7Genesis 3:19Ecclesiastes 9:5–6, 10Psalm 146:4Daniel 12:2John 11:11–14

Genesis says the man became a living soul when God's breath gave life to the dust. At death, Scripture describes a return to the earth, thoughts perishing, and the dead knowing nothing of the affairs of this life. Jesus and the prophets repeatedly use sleep as the picture of death when pointing toward resurrection.

Pattern: death is an enemy from which God raises people. The ordinary biblical picture is not a naturally conscious soul that cannot cease living.
A direct statement from Jesus

Can the soul die?

Ezekiel 18:4, 20Matthew 10:28

Ezekiel says the soul that sins shall die. Jesus warns that God is able to destroy both soul and body in Gehenna. Whatever else Gehenna means in a passage, Jesus' warning here is not that the soul is indestructible.

What this establishes: Scripture can speak of the whole person—including the soul—as subject to death and destruction.
The prophets' final-outcome language

Perish. Consume away. Stubble. Ashes.

Psalm 37:9–10, 20, 38Obadiah 16Malachi 4:1–3

Psalm 37 says the wicked perish and consume away. Obadiah describes the condemned as becoming "as though they had not been." Malachi pictures the final day as an oven: the wicked are stubble, the fire leaves neither root nor branch, and they become ashes.

Important: these images describe fire that succeeds in consuming what it burns.
Difficult text 1

Unquenchable fire

Matthew 3:12Mark 9:43–48Jeremiah 17:27Isaiah 66:24

In Scripture, a fire that cannot be quenched is a fire no one can stop before it finishes its work. Jeremiah warned that Jerusalem would burn with a fire that would not be quenched; Jerusalem is not still burning. John the Baptist says chaff is burned with unquenchable fire—chaff is consumed.

Jesus' language comes from Isaiah 66:24. Isaiah's scene concerns the carcases of rebels. The undying worm and unquenched fire describe complete disgrace and destruction of corpses, not immortal souls being preserved alive.

Difficult text 2

Eternal fire

Jude 72 Peter 2:6

Jude calls Sodom and Gomorrah an example of the vengeance of eternal fire. Peter tells what happened to that example: the cities were turned into ashes.

Scripture supplies its own example: the result of the fire is permanent even though the cities are not still burning.
Difficult text 3

Everlasting punishment

Matthew 25:462 Thessalonians 1:9Revelation 20:14

Matthew says the punishment is everlasting. The key question is: What is the punishment? Elsewhere Scripture calls it everlasting destruction and finally the second death. An everlasting result does not require an everlasting process of keeping the condemned alive.

Revelation defines its own symbol

The lake of fire is the second death

Revelation 20:14–15Revelation 21:8

Twice Revelation gives the reader a plain-language definition: the lake of fire is the second death. The first death is followed by resurrection. No resurrection is promised after the second death.

Interpretive guardrail: symbolic statements elsewhere in Revelation should be read in harmony with the book's explicit definition of the lake of fire.
Difficult text 4

Tormented day and night for ever and ever

Revelation 20:10Revelation 20:14–15Isaiah 34:9–10

Revelation 20:10 specifically names the devil, the beast, and the false prophet. The beast and false prophet are symbolic figures within Revelation. Just four verses later, the same vision names the lake of fire as the second death for the condemned.

Isaiah 34 describes Edom's smoke rising forever, while the following context pictures a devastated land rather than a landscape eternally on fire. The language communicates irreversible judgment.

Difficult text 5

The smoke of their torment ascends for ever and ever

Revelation 14:9–11Isaiah 34:8–10Revelation 18:8–10Revelation 19:3

Revelation borrows the prophetic language of permanent judgment. Babylon is destroyed, yet Revelation says her smoke rises forever. The image functions as a permanent memorial of judgment, not as proof that the destroyed city remains alive.

The passage does describe real suffering and “no rest day nor night.” That should not be softened. The narrower question is whether the condemned remain alive forever after judgment. Revelation's later definition remains: the lake of fire is the second death.

Difficult text 6

The rich man and Lazarus

Luke 16:19–31John 5:28–29John 11:11–14

The story uses vivid imagery: Abraham's bosom, conversation across a fixed gulf, and a request for a fingertip of water in flame. But Jesus' climax is in verse 31: if people will not hear Moses and the prophets, they will not be persuaded even if someone rises from the dead.

Reading rule: the story's moral warning should be read with Jesus' plain teaching that the dead await resurrection from the graves and that death is like sleep.
A translation issue worth noticing

One English word, several biblical terms

English Bibles have sometimes used “hell” for different words that do not all mean the same thing.

TermWhat to notice
SheolHebrew term often referring to the grave or realm of the dead.
HadesGreek term often corresponding to Sheol; Revelation 20:14 shows Hades is cast into the lake of fire, so they are not identical.
GehennaJesus' repeated final-judgment warning term; Matthew 10:28 describes destruction of both soul and body there.
TartarusA related verb occurs in 2 Peter 2:4 concerning fallen angels held for judgment.
Who possesses immortality?

Is eternal life automatic?

1 Timothy 6:15–16Romans 2:71 Corinthians 15:51–541 John 5:11–12

God alone inherently has immortality. Believers seek immortality, and at the resurrection the mortal puts it on. John says eternal life is in the Son; whoever does not have the Son does not have life.

This fits the larger pattern: immortality is God's gift to the redeemed, not an unavoidable condition that forces the lost to live forever.
Put the evidence together

What conclusion does the whole pattern support?

God's final judgment is real, terrible, just, and irreversible. The wicked are raised for judgment, experience the judgment of God, and are finally destroyed in the lake of fire—the second death. The results of that judgment are eternal. Scripture does not require the wicked to remain consciously alive forever in order for the punishment to be everlasting.

The repeated contrasts are life versus death, immortality versus mortality, preservation versus destruction, and salvation versus perishing.

Read together: Romans 6:23 — John 3:16 — Revelation 20:14. Ask one question: if eternal life is God's gift and the lake of fire is the second death, what must be added to the text in order to make the lost live forever?
