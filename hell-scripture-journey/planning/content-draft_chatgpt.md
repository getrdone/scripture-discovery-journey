# Hell Scripture Journey — full content draft

**Status:** content-approved draft; translation wording still requires passage-by-passage review before public lock.  
**Method:** Scripture compared with Scripture. No denominational labels in public-facing material.  
**Core question:** What does the Bible itself say about the final fate of the wicked—and does it teach never-ending conscious torment?

## Opening — what question is actually being asked?

The question is not whether the Bible teaches judgment, fire, punishment, or a terrible final consequence for sin. It plainly does.

The narrower question is this: **Does the Bible say that the lost remain alive and conscious forever while being tormented, or does it describe their punishment as death, destruction, consuming fire, and a final end?**

A sound answer should account for all of the language, including the passages that at first sound like endless torment.

---

## 1. Begin with the Bible's simplest contrast: life or death

Read **Romans 6:23** and **John 3:16**.

Romans places two outcomes opposite one another: the wages of sin are **death**, while God's gift is **eternal life** through Jesus Christ. John uses the same contrast: those who receive the Son have everlasting life; those who do not will **perish**.

That raises an important question. If every human being must live forever somewhere, why does Scripture repeatedly make *eternal life* the gift of salvation rather than the natural possession of everyone?

### What to notice

- Sin earns **death**.
- Salvation gives **eternal life**.
- The lost are said to **perish**.

This does not settle every difficult passage, but it establishes the Bible's basic vocabulary before later symbolic or figurative passages are examined.

**Compare:** Romans 2:7; 1 Timothy 6:16; 1 John 5:11–12.

---

## 2. What is a human being—and what happens at death?

Read **Genesis 2:7**. The text describes dust from the ground plus God's breath of life, and the man **became** a living soul. The verse does not describe an immortal soul being inserted into a temporary body.

Then compare **Genesis 3:19**, **Ecclesiastes 9:5–6, 10**, and **Psalm 146:4**.

The repeated picture is physical death followed by unconsciousness: a person returns to the earth; thoughts perish; the dead do not participate in the affairs of this life; there is no work, knowledge, or wisdom in the grave.

Other passages use the language of **sleep** for death, especially when pointing forward to resurrection: **Daniel 12:2; John 11:11–14; 1 Thessalonians 4:13–17**.

### What to notice

The Bible's ordinary description of death is not a second conscious life continuing somewhere else. Death is an enemy from which God raises people.

That matters because any interpretation of hell that requires the dead to remain naturally conscious should be tested against this larger biblical picture.

---

## 3. Can the soul die?

Read **Ezekiel 18:4, 20**: the soul that sins shall die.

Then read **Matthew 10:28**, where Jesus warns that God is able to **destroy both soul and body in Gehenna**.

Jesus does not describe the soul here as indestructible. The warning is severe precisely because God can bring the whole person to ruin.

### What to notice

Scripture warns of destruction of both body and soul. That is difficult to reconcile with the idea that the lost must remain indestructibly alive forever.

---

## 4. What does the Old Testament say the wicked finally become?

Read **Psalm 37:9–10, 20, 34, 38**; **Obadiah 16**; and **Malachi 4:1–3**.

The vocabulary is remarkably concrete:

- the wicked will be **cut off**;
- they will **perish**;
- they will consume away;
- they will be **as though they had not been**;
- the final day burns like an oven;
- the wicked are compared with **stubble**;
- they become **ashes**.

Malachi adds the striking phrase that the fire leaves them neither **root nor branch**.

### What to notice

The fire succeeds in consuming what it burns. The picture is not of fuel that miraculously remains alive forever in the flames.

---

## 5. What does Jesus mean by "unquenchable fire"?

Several passages use this expression: **Matthew 3:12; Mark 9:43–48**.

"Unquenchable" need not mean a fire that never goes out because its victims can never finish burning. In biblical usage, an unquenchable fire is a fire no one can stop before it completes its work.

Compare **Jeremiah 17:27**. God warned that Jerusalem's gates would be burned with a fire that "shall not be quenched." Jerusalem is not still burning today. The warning meant that once the judgment began, no human power would extinguish it before the destruction was complete.

The same principle helps with John the Baptist's statement in **Matthew 3:12**: the chaff is burned with unquenchable fire. Chaff is consumed by fire; it is not preserved by it.

### What about "their worm dieth not" in Mark 9?

Jesus is drawing from **Isaiah 66:24**. Isaiah's scene concerns the **carcases**—dead bodies—of those who rebelled against God. The undying worm and unquenched fire describe complete disgrace and destruction. The objects in the scene are corpses, not immortal conscious souls being kept alive.

### What to notice

The biblical images emphasize a judgment that cannot be stopped and that fully consumes what is condemned.

---

## 6. What does "eternal fire" mean when Scripture gives an example?

Read **Jude 7**. Sodom and Gomorrah are presented as an example of the vengeance of **eternal fire**.

Then let Scripture explain what happened to those cities. **2 Peter 2:6** says they were turned into **ashes** and made an example of what would happen to the ungodly.

The fire's result is eternal; the cities are not burning now.

This distinction—an eternal result rather than an eternally continuing process—appears elsewhere in biblical language. **Hebrews 9:12** speaks of "eternal redemption." Christ is not endlessly performing the act of redemption; the redemption accomplished has permanent effect.

### What to notice

"Eternal" can describe the permanence of what judgment accomplishes. Jude's own example is especially important because Scripture itself identifies what "eternal fire" did: it reduced the cities to ashes.

---

## 7. What is "eternal punishment" in Matthew 25:46?

Jesus says that the wicked go away into **everlasting punishment**, while the righteous enter **life eternal**.

The text says the punishment is eternal. It does not by itself say that the *act of punishing* must consist of endless conscious torment.

The rest of Scripture identifies the penalty for sin as death and the final judgment as destruction. **2 Thessalonians 1:9** calls it **everlasting destruction**. Revelation calls the lake of fire the **second death**.

The contrast in Matthew 25 is therefore naturally read as two permanent outcomes:

- eternal life;
- an irreversible punishment whose result is death and destruction.

### What to notice

The duration of the consequence is eternal. The question is what the punishment *is*. Other passages answer: death, destruction, consuming fire, and the second death.

---

## 8. The Bible names the lake of fire

Read **Revelation 20:14–15** and **Revelation 21:8**.

Twice the book gives its own definition: **the lake of fire is the second death**.

That definition should control the interpretation rather than being replaced by a different definition imported from elsewhere.

The first death is temporary because resurrection follows. The second death is different: it follows judgment and has no resurrection promised after it.

### What to notice

Revelation's own plain-language explanation of its fire imagery is **death**.

---

## 9. But Revelation 20:10 says "tormented day and night for ever and ever"

This is one of the strongest passages for the traditional view and should not be skipped.

Read the verse carefully. **Revelation 20:10** specifically names **the devil, the beast, and the false prophet**. Revelation frequently uses symbolic figures: the beast and false prophet represent powers rather than ordinary individual human beings.

The surrounding passage then distinguishes their fate from that of the resurrected wicked. In **Revelation 20:14–15**, the lake of fire into which death, Hades, and the condemned are cast is explicitly called **the second death**.

That creates an interpretive guardrail: symbolic apocalyptic language in verse 10 should not be used to erase the book's own definition in verses 14–15.

There is also an Old Testament precedent for "forever" language describing irreversible destruction rather than an endlessly ongoing process. **Isaiah 34:9–10** describes Edom's smoke going up "for ever," yet the following verses describe its land as a desolate place inhabited by wild creatures. The smoke language communicates permanent devastation.

### What to notice

Revelation 20 contains highly symbolic enemies and highly symbolic judgment imagery, but when the vision tells the reader what the lake of fire *means* for the condemned, it says: **the second death**.

---

## 10. What about Revelation 14:11—"the smoke of their torment ascendeth up for ever and ever"?

This is another serious passage.

Read **Revelation 14:9–11** together with **Isaiah 34:8–10**. Revelation's wording echoes Isaiah's judgment oracle against Edom, where smoke rises forever as a picture of complete and irreversible devastation.

Revelation itself later depicts Babylon's destruction with similar language. **Revelation 18:8–10, 18–19** portrays sudden fiery destruction, and **Revelation 19:3** says her smoke rises "for ever and ever." Yet Babylon is presented as fallen and destroyed, not maintained eternally as a functioning city undergoing conscious pain.

The phrase therefore fits the Bible's established judgment imagery: the smoke is an everlasting memorial of a judgment whose result cannot be reversed.

The passage also says the worshipers "have no rest day nor night" during the judgment scene. That is real suffering and should not be minimized. But the broader biblical question remains whether those people are kept alive forever. Revelation later calls their final end the second death.

### What to notice

The passage teaches dreadful judgment. The Old Testament source of its imagery and Revelation's own later definitions point to irreversible destruction rather than immortal life in torment.

---

## 11. What about the rich man and Lazarus in Luke 16?

Read **Luke 16:19–31** as a whole.

Several features signal that Jesus is using a story with vivid imagery rather than giving a geographical map of the afterlife:

- the saved are pictured at Abraham's bosom;
- the rich man and Abraham carry on a conversation across a fixed gulf;
- the rich man asks for a fingertip of water to cool his tongue in flame;
- the climax is not a description of hell but a warning that people who refuse **Moses and the prophets** will not be persuaded even if someone rises from the dead.

The central teaching lands in verse 31: God's revealed Word is sufficient, and unbelief is not solved merely by witnessing a miracle.

A parable or illustrative story should be interpreted consistently with the Bible's plain teaching about death and resurrection rather than used to overturn it.

Compare Jesus' own teaching in **John 5:28–29**, where the dead are in the graves until resurrection, and **John 11:11–14**, where He describes death as sleep.

### What to notice

Luke 16 contains a moral warning using dramatic imagery. Its climax concerns hearing God's Word, not constructing a doctrine of conscious existence between death and resurrection.

---

## 12. What do the words translated "hell" actually refer to?

English Bibles sometimes use one word—"hell"—for several different biblical terms.

### Sheol

The Hebrew **Sheol** commonly refers to the grave or realm of the dead. Both righteous and wicked people are described as going there in the Old Testament.

### Hades

Greek **Hades** often corresponds to Sheol. In **Revelation 20:14**, Hades itself is cast into the lake of fire, showing that Hades and the lake of fire are not identical.

### Gehenna

**Gehenna** is the term Jesus repeatedly uses in warnings about final judgment. In **Matthew 10:28**, He describes what God can do there as destroying both body and soul.

### Tartarus

A verbal form related to **Tartarus** appears in **2 Peter 2:4** concerning fallen angels being held for judgment. It is not the general biblical term for the final fate of lost humanity.

### What to notice

Treating every occurrence of the English word "hell" as though it described the same place, time, and event can blur distinctions Scripture itself makes.

---

## 13. When does final punishment occur?

A repeated biblical pattern places reward and punishment in connection with resurrection and judgment, not immediately at death.

Compare:

- **John 5:28–29** — resurrection of life and resurrection of judgment;
- **Matthew 13:40–43** — judgment at "the end of this world";
- **2 Peter 2:9** — the unjust are reserved unto the day of judgment to be punished;
- **Revelation 20:5–15** — resurrection, judgment, then the lake of fire.

### What to notice

The final punishment belongs to the judgment after resurrection. This fits the Bible's description of the dead as sleeping until they are raised.

---

## 14. Does God give immortality to everyone?

The Bible repeatedly treats immortality as God's possession and God's gift, not humanity's automatic condition.

Read **1 Timothy 6:15–16**, **Romans 2:7**, **1 Corinthians 15:51–54**, and **1 John 5:11–12**.

Paul says believers **seek for** immortality. At the resurrection, "this mortal" puts on immortality. John says eternal life is in the Son, and whoever does not have the Son does not have life.

### What to notice

The saved receive immortality through Christ. Scripture does not need the lost to possess immortal life in order for God's judgment to be eternal and final.

---

## 15. Put the vocabulary side by side

Read these descriptions together rather than allowing one difficult phrase to stand alone:

| Passage | Final-outcome language |
|---|---|
| Romans 6:23 | death |
| John 3:16 | perish |
| Matthew 10:28 | destroy both soul and body |
| Psalm 37:20 | perish / consume away |
| Obadiah 16 | as though they had not been |
| Malachi 4:1–3 | stubble / ashes |
| Matthew 3:12 | chaff burned with unquenchable fire |
| 2 Thessalonians 1:9 | everlasting destruction |
| 2 Peter 2:6 | Sodom reduced to ashes as an example |
| Revelation 20:14 | second death |
| Revelation 21:8 | second death |

The cumulative pattern is difficult to miss: Scripture's ordinary language for the final fate of the wicked is **death and destruction**, not everlasting life in misery.

---

## 16. So what does the Bible's evidence support?

Taken together, the passages support this conclusion:

**God's final judgment is real, terrible, just, and irreversible. The wicked are raised for judgment, experience the judgment of God, and are finally destroyed in the lake of fire—the second death. The results of that judgment are eternal. Scripture does not require the wicked to remain consciously alive forever in order for the punishment to be everlasting.**

The repeated contrasts are life versus death, immortality versus mortality, preservation versus destruction, and salvation versus perishing.

That also explains why eternal life is consistently presented as the gift of God through Christ.

---

## 17. Boundaries: what this study does and does not claim

This study does **not** deny the reality of divine judgment, the suffering connected with judgment, or the solemnity of Jesus' warnings.

It does not attempt to calculate how long any individual's experience of final punishment lasts before the second death.

It does not claim that every fire image is merely metaphorical. Rather, it asks Scripture to define the final *result* of judgment.

And it does not make one symbolic apocalyptic phrase cancel the broad, repeated biblical vocabulary of death, destruction, perishing, ashes, and the second death.
