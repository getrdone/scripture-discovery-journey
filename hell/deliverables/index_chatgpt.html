What Does the Bible Really Say About Hell?

Many people picture hell as a place where the lost stay alive forever in fire. But is that what the Bible actually says? Start with a simpler question: What does Scripture say the final result of sin is?

Follow the words life, death, perish, destroy, fire, and second death. Then compare the difficult passages with the clear ones.

1. The story begins with life — and a warning about death

Genesis 2:7, 16–17; 3:19, 22–24

Adam becomes a living being when God gives him life. After sin, he returns to dust. Access to the tree of life is barred so fallen humanity will not “live for ever.”

That creates an important starting point. In Genesis, endless life is not presented as something every human automatically possesses. Life depends on God.

God warns, “thou shalt surely die” (Genesis 2:17). The serpent answers, “Ye shall not surely die” (Genesis 3:4). The rest of the study can test which claim the later Bible writers repeat.

2. What are the wages of sin?

Ezekiel 18:4, 20

Both verses say plainly: “the soul that sinneth, it shall die.”

Romans 6:23

Paul places two outcomes opposite each other: the wages of sin are death; God's gift is eternal life through Jesus Christ.

Notice the contrast. Paul does not contrast eternal happiness with eternal life in torment. His words are death and eternal life.

3. Is every soul already immortal?

1 Timothy 6:15–16

God is described as the One “who only hath immortality.”

1 Corinthians 15:51–54

At the resurrection, the saved “put on immortality.”

If immortality is something God's people put on at resurrection, it is not something Scripture needs to assume every person already has by nature.

4. Where are the dead before resurrection?

Ecclesiastes 9:5–6, 10; Psalm 146:4

The dead “know not any thing”; their thoughts perish; the grave is described as without work, knowledge, or wisdom.

John 11:11–14

Jesus says Lazarus sleeps, then tells the disciples plainly that Lazarus is dead.

1 Thessalonians 4:13–17

Paul comforts grieving believers with the return of Christ and the resurrection of those who “sleep in Jesus.”

The Bible's hope is not that death secretly leaves everyone fully alive. Its great hope is resurrection.

5. Listen to the Bible's ordinary words for the fate of the lost

John 3:16

The two outcomes are to perish or have everlasting life.

Matthew 10:28

Jesus warns that God is able to destroy both soul and body in hell.

Psalm 37:9–10, 20, 38

The wicked are cut off, perish, vanish like smoke, and finally are “no more.”

Malachi 4:1–3

The final day burns like an oven. The wicked are burned up and become ashes.

These passages use different pictures, but the result points in one direction: evil does not receive everlasting life. It comes to an end.

6. Then what does “eternal fire” mean?

Jude 7

Sodom and Gomorrah are given as an example of “eternal fire.”

2 Peter 2:6

Peter says those cities were turned into ashes, making them an example of what happens to the ungodly.

The example helps define the phrase. The fire's judgment is eternal in result. Sodom was completely destroyed; the city is not still burning today.

7. Revelation gives the lake of fire a name

Revelation 20:5–15; 21:4, 8

After the resurrection and judgment of the lost, the lake of fire appears. Twice Revelation calls it “the second death.” Then the Bible looks beyond judgment to a world where death itself is gone.

If the first death can be followed by resurrection, the “second death” is different: Scripture presents no later resurrection from it. The punishment is final.

8. What about the verses that sound like endless torment?

A fair study cannot skip them. Read Mark 9:43–48, Matthew 25:46, Luke 16:19–31, Revelation 14:9–11, Revelation 20:10, and Isaiah 66:24.

Three checks keep the comparison honest: What kind of writing is this? What Old Testament language is being reused? And does the passage require the lost to possess immortality when clearer texts say immortality is God's gift?

Jesus' language about the worm and unquenchable fire points back to Isaiah 66:24. Isaiah describes dead bodies being consumed, not immortal people being kept alive. That background matters before assigning a different meaning to Jesus' quotation.

Revelation uses highly symbolic judgment language. Compare its smoke imagery with Isaiah 34:9–10, where Edom's smoke rises “for ever” although the prophecy describes a completed desolation. Then keep Revelation's own definition in view: the lake of fire is “the second death” (Revelation 20:14).

The answer the passages build toward

The Bible presents eternal life as God's gift, not the natural possession of every human being. The dead await resurrection. The lost face real judgment and real fire, but Revelation names the final result the second death. The punishment is everlasting because its result cannot be reversed — not because God keeps the lost alive forever in order to torment them.

This does not make judgment unreal or small. It means sin, suffering, death, and rebellion actually end.
